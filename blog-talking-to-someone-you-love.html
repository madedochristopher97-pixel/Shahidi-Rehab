How to talk to someone you love about getting help

If someone close to you is struggling with alcohol or drugs, the hardest part is often the first conversation. Here is how to prepare for it.

For families19 November 2026

Most families tell us the same thing: they knew something was wrong for a long time before they said anything. Fear of making it worse keeps people quiet, and the silence itself becomes part of the problem.

There is no perfect script. But there are things that make the conversation easier to start, and easier to come back to if the first attempt does not go well.

Choose the moment carefully

Do not raise it when the person is intoxicated, hungover, or in the middle of a crisis. Nothing said in those moments tends to stick. Look for a quiet, private time when neither of you is rushing somewhere, and when you are calm enough to listen rather than argue.

Lead with what you have noticed, not with labels

Words like "addict" or "alcoholic" usually put people on the defensive, and the conversation turns into a debate about whether the label fits. Describe what you have actually seen instead.

"You have missed work three times this month and I am worried about you" is harder to argue with than "you have a drinking problem". Stay with specific, factual things, and say plainly that they come from concern.

Expect denial, and do not treat it as failure

Denial is common, and it is usually fear or shame rather than dishonesty. The person may get angry, change the subject, or promise to cut down on their own. That does not mean the conversation was wasted. Many people need to hear the same concern several times, from people who do not give up on them, before they act on it.

Have something concrete to offer

Vague encouragement is easy to deflect. A specific next step is harder to brush off, and it takes the weight of organising things off the person who is struggling.

It helps to know, before the conversation, what treatment would actually involve: what an assessment is, roughly how long a stay lasts, what it costs. You can call us beforehand and ask all of that yourself, without naming anybody and without committing to anything.

Set boundaries, with care

Protecting yourself is not the same as giving up on someone. You can decide that you will not cover debts, lie to employers, or allow drinking in the house, and still make it clear that you will support treatment. Say what you will and will not do, then hold to it. Boundaries that keep shifting teach people that nothing has really changed.

Look after yourself too

Living alongside someone else's addiction is exhausting, and the worry does not stop just because they agree to get help. Our family team offers confidential consultations for spouses, parents and adult children, whether or not your loved one is ready. You do not have to wait for them to say yes before you get support.
